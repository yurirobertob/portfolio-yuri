A subscription sports-analytics product, designed and built solo — from the first wireframe to the cron jobs running it in production.

The problem

Sports-betting analysis in Brazil runs on tipsters — people who read stats by hand and post picks with no track record anyone can audit. The category is loud, hype-driven, and full of screenshots of wins with the losses cropped out.

GoScout replaces that with a statistical engine that publishes automatically and keeps a public, unedited record of every result. But that creates the actual design problem: the product only works if someone trusts a number they didn't calculate themselves. The interface has to earn credibility in a category where nobody has any — and it has to gate paid content without ever feeling like a bait-and-switch.

And it's used one-handed, on a phone, mid-match.

Design decisions

Four calls that shaped the product

Blur the number, never the noun

The first paywall I built blurred entire cards. It tested badly with the people I showed it to — a wall of grey rectangles reads as "this product is hiding something," and people left before understanding what they'd even be paying for.

So I rewrote the rule: free users see the full shape of every screen — team names, matches, layout, labels — and only the specific paid value is obscured. You can always see what you'd get. You just can't read the number yet.

Trade-off This gives away more of the product than a hard wall does, and some users will be satisfied with the free layer alone. I took that hit deliberately: an understood paywall converts better than an opaque one, and it doesn't poison trust in a category where trust is the entire product.

A floating nav pill — and the bug that only existed on real hardware

I designed the primary mobile navigation as a floating pill anchored above the safe area rather than a full-width bar, so content runs full-bleed underneath it and the chrome stays light on a small screen.

It looked perfect in the browser's device emulator and broke on an actual iPhone. Two separate causes: Safari silently fails on fixed positioning when the offset combines calc() with env(safe-area-inset), and a Tailwind arbitrary-value class with two tokens (justify-[safe_center]) never compiles to CSS at all — it just produces nothing, with no error.

What changed after Every UI change now ships with a check on physical hardware before I call it done. Emulators verify layout; they don't verify the platform.

Dark-first, because of where the product is actually used

People open GoScout at night, on a couch, with a match on. So dark isn't the alternate theme here — it's the origin. I designed the palette dark-first and derived the light theme from it, rather than the usual inversion.

The system came out of studying how FotMob and Betano handle dense sports data: near-black surfaces, one brand green carrying identity, and semantic colour reserved for meaning (a distinct accent marks the high-variance bet profile, so it can never be confused with the safe one at a glance).

Trade-off I wanted a display typeface with real personality, and the reference used a proprietary one that isn't licensed for redistribution. I used a freely licensed family instead and pushed the character through weight and scale. Shipping legally beat matching the reference exactly.

Different density for scanning versus deciding

Two screens in the same product have opposite jobs. The compact grid exists to let someone scan dozens of matches in seconds — tight rows, colour-coded probability, sortable columns. The detail view exists for the single moment a user decides whether to trust one number enough to act on it, so it slows down and gives that number room.

Same design system, deliberately different density. The rule I kept coming back to was Krug's — don't make me think — applied per surface rather than globally.

Payments: the flow most designers never touch

GoScout is a real subscription business, so I designed and implemented the entire commercial surface — not just a pricing page.

  • Contextual checkout entry. The upgrade prompt appears where a user actually hits the limit, not only on a separate pricing route. The moment of intent and the moment of offer are the same moment.
  • Skeleton-first gating. Locked screens render their real skeleton immediately and then resolve into a lock state, instead of hard-redirecting on load. A redirect feels like an error; this feels like a door.
  • Account and subscription management. Plan status, renewal date, and self-serve cancellation — visible, not buried behind a support email.
  • Attribution that survives checkout. I built the click-ID capture and signup attribution through the middleware so paid-acquisition data isn't lost between the ad and the subscription.

That last one came from a real finding: a genuine sale was invisible in the ads platform because attribution broke on the way through. Design decisions in a funnel are only as good as the measurement proving them.

What's behind the interface

The screens sit on a system I also built. A scheduled engine ingests fixtures, team and player statistics, lineups and market odds across 100+ leagues, inside a hard budget of 75,000 API requests per day enforced in code — jobs carry per-run caps, because going over quota would take the whole product down for the rest of the day. Live matches get a dedicated one-minute refresh so real-time data never competes with historical backfill.

The part I'd defend hardest is a product principle, not a technical one: not publishing beats publishing something weak. Every market has to clear validation against historical results before it's allowed to appear. Several stay switched off in production today because they never cleared it — including one I wanted to ship. In a product whose entire value is a credible track record, the discipline to show less is the feature.

Production reality

The payment webhook that never made a sound

This is the part I'd point a hiring manager to first. Paying subscribers weren't getting access, and the obvious explanation was wrong.

Symptom

Two subscribers paid and got nothing. It looked like a repeat of an auth-token bug I'd already found and fixed once. It wasn't.

Investigation

I checked the audit table that logs every inbound webhook before processing. It had zero rows — not since the integration existed. That ruled out the handler entirely: the code was never being reached.

Root cause

The webhook URL registered with the payment provider pointed at the bare apex domain, which the host 308-redirects to www at the routing layer. The provider doesn't follow redirects on webhook calls. Every request died before touching application code — no error, no failed auth, no log line, because nothing ever arrived.

Fix and verification

Corrected the URL to the canonical host, then verified with a real webhook POST end to end: 200 response, event applied, row written.

The systemic fix

A wrong URL breaks silently again — DNS changes, someone edits a dashboard. So instead of just fixing the URL, I shipped a watchdog that monitors for silence rather than errors: if no webhook lands for 24 hours, it alerts me. That covers this failure and every future one with the same symptom, without needing to predict the cause.

What this project actually demonstrates

That I close the loop. The paywall isn't a mockup of a paywall — it's connected to a webhook that grants access, an account page that reflects it, and an alert that fires when it breaks. Design decisions are cheap until something has to work at 2am for someone who paid.

That I debug at the right layer. Re-reading the handler code twice would never have found that incident. It took forming a hypothesis, checking evidence, and being willing to conclude the problem was outside the app entirely.

That I build for the next failure. Monitoring silence instead of errors is a small idea that covers a whole class of problems I haven't seen yet.

On how it was built: I worked in close pair-programming with Claude Code throughout — the engine, the incident fix, and the watchdog included. I'm explicit about that because it's a real part of how I ship this much scope alone, and because knowing what to build, what to reject, and what a wrong answer looks like is the part that doesn't automate.
