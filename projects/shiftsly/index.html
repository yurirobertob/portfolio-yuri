Where this came from

I lived in London between 2022 and 2023, and I kept meeting the same situation: cleaning-team managers running their entire operation out of WhatsApp threads. Who's working Tuesday, who's covering a sick day, which client site, what everyone gets paid — all of it in chat scrollback.

It doesn't scale past a handful of people, none of it is structured data, and nobody has visibility into who's covering what. The tools that exist — ZenMaid, Jobber — are either not cleaning-specific or are built and priced for operations much larger than the 5-to-20-person crews that make up most of the market.

The design constraint that decided everything: the buyer checks this standing in a client's driveway between jobs, on a phone, with one hand. Not at a desk. Every subsequent decision falls out of that.

Design decisions

Designing for a driveway, not a desk

The schedule is a carousel, not a grid

The obvious solution for a weekly schedule is a dense desktop-style grid — days across, people down. It's the pattern every scheduling tool uses, and on a phone held in one hand it's unusable: you either pinch-zoom or you scroll in two directions at once.

I built the core view as a swipeable weekly carousel instead: one day at a time at full width, thumb-reachable, with the week as context above it. The information architecture underneath matches how a manager actually thinks — teams → employees → weekly shifts → status — rather than borrowing a generic calendar-app data model.

Trade-off You lose the whole-week glance that a grid gives you for free. I accepted that because the frequent job is "who's covering today and is anyone missing," not "audit the full week" — and I kept a wider layout for the desk sessions where that second job actually happens.

No passwords, on purpose

Sign-in runs on Google OAuth or a magic link. There is no password field anywhere in the product.

For a non-technical buyer signing up on a phone between jobs, every field is a place to abandon — and every password is a future support ticket I'd have to answer personally. Removing the field removes both. It's a small interface decision with a direct effect on activation and on how much support load a solo founder can absorb.

Trade-off Magic links depend on email deliverability, which becomes a hard dependency on the transactional email provider. I judged that risk smaller than the drop-off and support cost of password auth.

Priced in cleaners, not seats

Three tiers, and all of them are labelled by headcount — number of cleaners — rather than the per-seat model most SaaS borrows by default. A cleaning-team manager thinks in "how many people do I have," never in "how many licences do I need."

The free tier goes up to five cleaners and is a genuinely usable product, not a crippled demo. That forced the interface to hold up at zero revenue and made the upgrade prompt feel earned rather than extracted.

Closing the loop with real users

Rather than guessing at friction, I built a usability-test flow into the product itself: a short multi-step form with NPS scoring, reviewed through an internal admin panel. Real users rate real friction points, and that feeds directly into what gets redesigned next.

I did the same thing for the business metrics. Instead of checking dashboards by hand, I built a scheduled agent that pulls live signals from Stripe and the product database twice a day and pushes a digest to me. It's a small piece of growth instrumentation, built for an audience of one — but it's the same signal-to-action loop any growth team runs.

What this project actually demonstrates

That I can find the problem, not just solve the given one. Nobody briefed Shiftsly. It came from noticing the same broken workflow repeatedly and checking whether the existing tools actually served that segment. That's the part of product design that happens before any screen exists.

That I make trade-offs and can defend them. Every decision above cost something. The carousel gave up the week-glance. Passwordless auth took on a deliverability dependency. A real free tier gave away real value. I'd make all three again, and I can tell you why.

That I own commercial surfaces. Pricing model, checkout, subscription lifecycle — live on Stripe, not sketched.

On how it was built: auth, billing, the scheduling core and the growth agent were built in close pair-programming with Claude Code, from schema design through UI. It's how one person covers this much surface area without the quality falling over.
