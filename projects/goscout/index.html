UX / UI Case Study

GoScout: Designing a Mobile-First Product Around Trust and a Paywall

Role: Product designer & front-end — IA, UI system, dark mode, paywall UX, checkout flow Surfaces: Mobile app shell, feed, match pages, subscription & account, checkout Status: Live subscription product since July 2026

The design problem

GoScout gives users automated sports-betting analysis, but the product only works if people trust a number they didn't calculate themselves. The UI had to do two conflicting jobs at once: read as calm and credible on a category (sports betting) that's usually loud and hype-driven, and clearly gate paid content without ever feeling like a bait-and-switch. Most users open it on a phone, mid-match, with one hand.

Key UI decisions

Mobile nav Paywall pattern Dark mode Checkout UX Density

Bottom navigation as a floating pill, not a bar: I designed the primary nav as a floating pill anchored to the safe area rather than a full-width bar, so it reads lighter on small screens and keeps content full-bleed underneath it. Getting this right took real-device testing, not just a simulator: Safari on iOS silently breaks fixed positioning combined with calc() and env(safe-area-inset) together, and Tailwind's arbitrary-value bracket syntax for a two-token class (justify-[safe_center]) doesn't compile at all. Both only showed up on a real iPhone, not in dev tools' device emulator, which is why every UI change since ships with a real-device check before I call it done.

Paywall as a teaser, not a wall: free users see the full shape of every screen: names, matches, layout. What's blurred is only the number that has to be paid for, never the label or the context around it. That one rule (blur the value, never the noun next to it) came out of testing early versions where blurring whole cards just felt like the product was hiding something, and users bounced before ever seeing what they'd pay for.

Dark mode as the default assumption: the product gets used at night, on a couch, watching a match. Every surface was designed dark-first, with light mode as the derived palette, not the other way around, because that's the actual usage context.

Information density tuned per surface: the guiding rule across the whole system was Krug's "don't make me think": a compact grid view for scanning many picks fast, and a slower, spaced-out layout for the one screen where a user decides whether to trust a single number enough to act on it. Same design system, deliberately different density depending on whether the screen's job is scanning or deciding.

Checkout & subscription UX

This is the part of the product closest to "payments experience": GoScout runs a real subscription flow end to end through a Brazilian payment provider (Hubla, the local equivalent of Stripe for this market), and I designed every screen in that funnel:

  • Checkout entry points that stay contextual, offered right where a free user hits the paywall, not just from one generic pricing page.
  • Account & subscription management screens where a user can see plan status, renewal date, and cancel without hunting for a support email.
  • Skeleton-first paywall screens instead of a hard redirect on load: locked content renders its real skeleton immediately, then swaps to a lock state, so the page never feels broken or slow, it feels intentionally gated.
  • Attribution-aware flows: I worked with the team's marketing pixel setup so the checkout UI never gets in the way of the tracking a paid-acquisition funnel actually depends on.

What this demonstrates

Designing gated/paywalled products

Deciding exactly what to hide and what to reveal on a freemium surface, and testing that decision against real user drop-off, not just intuition.

Real subscription/checkout UX

End-to-end design of a live payment funnel: entry points, checkout, account management, and cancellation, on a real payment provider.

Mobile-first, verified on real devices

A UI system built for one-handed, phone-first use, with a habit of testing on actual hardware after being burned by simulator-only bugs.

Systemized visual language

Dark mode as the default, consistent density rules across surfaces, and a documented "don't make me think" principle applied deliberately, not just aesthetics.
